COMMUNICATION · READER PICK · 2007

The Curse of Knowledge makes experts sound like Morse code

Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die · Chip Heath and Dan Heath
2026-11-27 Entry ↗
634 words

Core Idea

Ever notice how the smartest specialist in the room is often the hardest person to understand? We usually blame laziness or bad slides, yet in Made to Stick, Stanford professor Chip Heath and business educator Dan Heath argue the real culprit is cognitive: the "Curse of Knowledge," a bias first named by behavioral economists Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber in 1989.

Once you understand a strategy, architecture, or market deeply, your brain loses the ability to un-know it and imagine how the world looks to someone hearing it for the first time. Experts speak in compressed abstractions—"synergies," "platform leverage," "end-to-end orchestration"—that sound crystal clear in their own heads because they unconsciously supply all the concrete context underneath. Their audience, lacking that hidden scaffolding, hears noise. The Heath brothers argue you cannot simply will the curse away; you have to translate abstract expertise back into concrete, sensory language—the heart of their SUCCESs framework (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories).

Example from the Book

In the book's Introduction, the Heath brothers anchor the bias in a 1990 Stanford psychology dissertation by Elizabeth Newton. Newton paired participants into "tappers" and "listeners." Each tapper picked a familiar tune from a list of 25 classics—such as "Happy Birthday to You" or "The Star-Spangled Banner"—and tapped its rhythm on a table while the listener tried to name the song.

Across 120 tapped songs, listeners guessed correctly just 3 times—a dismal 2.5% hit rate. Yet before listeners guessed, tappers predicted they would nail the song 50% of the time, off by a factor of twenty. Why the gulf? While tapping, tappers could not stop hearing the full melody and lyrics booming inside their heads; to them, the song was unmistakable. Listeners heard only a baffling string of erratic knocks, like broken Morse code. Every strategy memo, engineering spec, or board deck written by an insider risks the same trap: the author hears the symphony while the room hears taps.

Argue For

Treating communication failure as a structural cognitive bias—rather than a character flaw—explains why smart organizations constantly misalign. CEOs spend months debating a turnaround plan and then announce a three-word slogan at an all-hands, baffled when frontline teams shrug and keep doing yesterday's work. Product engineers ship interfaces that feel "intuitive" only to people who already know the data model.

Because expertise is irreversible, good intentions never cure the curse; only concrete translation does. Concrete images, vivid constraints, and specific stories act as a shared language that both novices and experts process on equal footing. John F. Kennedy's 1961 pledge—landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth before the decade's end—stuck not because it dumbed down rocket science, but because it replaced abstract aerospace jargon with a sensory target everyone could picture.

Argue Against

Concreteness is not a universal solvent, and pushing it too far can backfire. Among peers who already share deep domain fluency—surgeons in an operating room, traders on a desk, or distributed-systems engineers debugging an outage—dense jargon and high-level abstractions are not a curse; they are high-bandwidth compression that saves vital time and precision.

Oversimplifying complex realities into sticky slogans also carries real strategic risk. A memorable soundbite can flatten crucial edge cases, conceal statistical uncertainty, or lock an organization into an overly rigid framing when conditions change. Worse, as critics of persuasion frameworks note, the same sticky mechanics that help good ideas travel—vivid anecdotes and emotional punch—spread urban legends and misleading narratives just as effortlessly. The real leadership skill is not abandoning abstraction, but knowing when you are speaking to fellow tappers versus listeners.

“Once we know something, we find it hard to imagine what it was like not to know it. Our knowledge has 'cursed' us. And it becomes difficult for us to share our knowledge with others, because we can't readily re-create our listeners' state of mind.”

— Chip Heath and Dan Heath, Made to Stick (2007), Introduction

Inclusive institutions—not geography or culture—make nations rich

Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty · Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson
2026-11-26 Entry ↗
618 words

Core Idea

Why are some countries rich and others poor? Standard dinner-table explanations blame geography (tropics versus temperate zones), climate, natural resources, or national culture. In Why Nations Fail—building on institutional research that helped earn Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson (with Simon Johnson) the 2024 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences—the authors argue those stories get causality backward: long-run prosperity is decided by human-made institutions.

Acemoglu and Robinson divide societies into two types. Inclusive economic institutions protect property rights, enforce impartial contracts, and let anyone start a business or patent an idea, backed by inclusive political institutions that distribute power broadly with checks and balances. Extractive institutions concentrate power in a narrow elite that rigs markets, protects monopolies, and skims wealth from the majority. Because elites fear "creative destruction"—new technologies and upstarts that can topple incumbents—extractive regimes actively block innovation even when it would expand the whole economy.

Example from the Book

Chapter 1 ("So Close and Yet So Different") opens on a single border fence cutting through the desert city of Nogales. North of the wire sits Nogales, Arizona; just steps south lies Nogales, Sonora. Both halves share the exact same geography, climate, disease environment, and largely the same cultural heritage and ancestry.

Yet the gap across the fence is stark. On the authors' 2012 figures, the average household in Nogales, Arizona earns about $30,000 a year, most adults hold a high school diploma, residents rely on public sanitation, law and order, and Medicare after 65, and life expectancy is high. Steps away in Nogales, Sonora, average household income is roughly one-third as large, most adults lack a high school degree, many teenagers are out of school, infant mortality is higher, and crime and corruption weigh on daily commerce. What changed at the fence was not the soil or the people—it was the institutional rulebook: U.S. property rights, courts, and electoral accountability versus Mexico's legacy of colonial monopolies, weak rule of law, and politically entrenched elites.

Argue For

Centering prosperity on institutions explains sudden historical divergences that geography and culture cannot. The Korean Peninsula shares one climate, language, and centuries of common history, yet after its post-1945 partition, South Korea built market-oriented institutions (and later pluralistic democracy) while North Korea built a closed, extractive command state—producing a staggering gap in living standards within two generations.

For MBA leaders and capital allocators, the lesson extends straight to emerging markets and corporate strategy: headline growth under crony rules rarely compounds into frontier innovation because insecure property rights and political favoritism choke off broad entrepreneurship. Even in 17th-century England, Acemoglu and Robinson show, it took the Glorious Revolution of 1688 to curb royal monopolies, empower Parliament, and unlock the Industrial Revolution.

Argue Against

Critics such as Jeffrey Sachs (in a sharp 2012 Foreign Affairs review) and Francis Fukuyama argue that Why Nations Fail turns a vital insight into a monocausal dogma. First, China's rise poses an awkward puzzle: under an authoritarian, politically "extractive" Communist Party, China lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty and scaled world-class infrastructure and frontier manufacturing. Acemoglu and Robinson counter that authoritarian "catch-up growth" eventually stalls without political pluralism—yet China has sustained industrial upgrading longer than early institutionalists expected.

Skeptics also note that dismissing geography and administrative state capacity understates real traps: landlocked nations without navigable ports, severe malaria burdens, or weak bureaucracies face steep headwinds that formal laws alone cannot erase. Moreover, East Asian tigers—such as 1960s South Korea before democratization, and high-income Singapore—built strong, centralized state capacity and industrial momentum without Western-style multiparty pluralism.

“Nations fail today because their extractive economic institutions do not create the incentives needed for people to save, invest, and innovate. Extractive political institutions support these economic institutions by cementing the power of those who benefit from the extraction.”

— Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson, Why Nations Fail (2012), Chapter 13

GEOPOLITICS · FT 2025 SHORTLIST

Why engineering states outbuild lawyerly societies

Breakneck: China's Quest to Engineer the Future · Dan Wang
2026-11-25 Entry ↗
552 words

Core Idea

Most commentary reduces U.S.–China rivalry to democracy versus authoritarianism, or capitalism versus state socialism. In Breakneck, technology analyst Dan Wang proposes a sharper institutional lens: China is an "engineering state," whereas the United States has become a "lawyerly society."

In an engineering state, leaders trained in technical disciplines treat the entire nation—from bridges and battery factories to demographics—as a physical system to optimize and build at scale. In a lawyerly society, elites trained in law prize procedure, rights, and litigation, wielding rules less to construct than to constrain. Neither model holds a monopoly on competence: engineering states deliver tangible physical abundance at astonishing speed, yet treat citizens as statistical inputs and inflict severe harm when unconstrained; lawyerly societies protect individual liberty and check abuses, yet let procedural vetoes paralyze basic public works.

Example from the Book

In 2008, both China and California launched ambitious ~800-mile high-speed rail efforts. China broke ground on the Beijing–Shanghai High-Speed Railway in April 2008 and opened it in June 2011—taking just over three years at a cost of roughly $36 billion (per Wang), and carrying 1.35 billion passengers over its first decade.

In November 2008, California voters approved Proposition 1A for a comparable ~800-mile statewide network (anchoring a ~500-mile San Francisco–Los Angeles Phase 1 spine). Nearly two decades on, entangled in environmental litigation, fragmented permitting, and local veto points, California still has no operational high-speed rail service while Phase 1 cost estimates have climbed as high as $128 billion. Wang notes that China's construction bias reaches even its poorest interior: mountainous Guizhou province built towering gorge bridges and expressways at breakneck pace, while U.S. cities struggle to permit neighborhood bike lanes.

Argue For

Viewing statecraft through elite training illuminates industrial dynamics that ideology alone misses. In 2002, eight of the nine members of China's Politburo Standing Committee held engineering degrees—including General Secretary Hu Jintao in hydraulic engineering, alongside geologist-trained Premier Wen Jiabao—and later leader Xi Jinping studied chemical engineering. By contrast, legal training dominates Washington: roughly half the U.S. Senate and a third of the House hold law degrees, and every Democratic presidential and vice-presidential nominee from 1984 to 2020 attended law school.

An engineering mindset excels at mobilizing capital, supply chains, and talent around physical transformation—explaining how China scaled solar panels, electric vehicles, and grid capacity so rapidly, and why America's own postwar engineering era gave way after 1970 to procedural bottlenecks that now stall clean energy transmission and housing.

Argue Against

Treating a nation like an engineering problem turns catastrophic when leaders apply the same sledgehammer to human lives. As Wang documents in chapters on the one-child policy and zero-Covid lockdowns, an engineering state unconstrained by due process or citizen consent views people as aggregate variables—enforcing coercive birth limits and sealing whole cities with rigid, top-down force.

Nor is legalism purely obstructionist. America's post-1960s procedural turn—sparked by highway-bulldozed neighborhoods, polluted waterways, and unchecked corporate power—established vital environmental and civil-rights guardrails. Moreover, building at any cost creates steep economic hangovers: debt-laden provinces like Guizhou are dotted with underutilized concrete and bridges, while America's rule-of-law openness still anchors global leadership in software and biotech.

“The starkest contrast between the two countries is the competition that will define the twenty-first century: an American elite, made up of mostly lawyers, excelling at obstruction, versus a Chinese technocratic class, made up of mostly engineers, that excels at construction.”

— Dan Wang, Breakneck (2025), Introduction

GEOPOLITICS · FT 2021 SHORTLIST

Shadow commodity traders act as markets' visible hand

The World for Sale: Money, Power, and the Traders Who Barter the Earth's Resources · Javier Blas and Jack Farchy
2026-11-24 Entry ↗
584 words

Core Idea

Global capitalism runs on physical goods—crude oil, copper, cobalt, and wheat—yet almost no consumer knows the companies that actually move them. In The World for Sale, journalists Javier Blas and Jack Farchy examine how a handful of independent commodity trading houses, led by Vitol, Glencore, Trafigura, and Cargill, quietly became indispensable gears of the world economy.

Unlike oil majors or mining giants, pure-play traders rarely care whether commodity prices rise or fall. Instead, they are master arbitrageurs who profit from price disparities across geography, product quality, and time—buying cheap surplus in one port, blending grades on ships at sea, or storing cargoes in supertankers until futures prices justify delivery. Because physical shortages and geopolitical shocks widen those spreads, market turmoil is their growth engine. Operating largely outside public equity markets, these firms act simultaneously as logistics operators, shadow bankers, and informal diplomats wherever state supply chains break down.

Example from the Book

In early 2011, during the Libyan Civil War, rebel forces in Benghazi held vast oil reserves in eastern Libya yet faced a crippling paradox: they were running out of gasoline, diesel, and fuel oil because Muammar Gaddafi's loyalists still held the country's main operational refineries. With conventional banks unwilling to extend credit to an unrecognized rebel council, Western governments struggled to keep rebel-held cities powered and vehicles moving.

Into the breach stepped Ian Taylor, the chief executive of Vitol, the world's largest independent oil trader. Flying into Benghazi aboard a small private jet—with a NATO drone chaperoning a thousand feet below—Taylor negotiated directly with rebel leaders and agreed on a handshake to supply roughly $1 billion worth of refined fuel. Vitol effectively acted as both lifeline and lender, deferring repayment until the rebels won and could settle the tab in crude oil. Even after Gaddafi's forces blew up a key pipeline, Vitol honored the deal and was repaid once the regime fell.

Argue For

Independent commodity traders solve a coordination problem that neither governments nor risk-averse multinationals handle well: matching physical supply to acute scarcity under extreme uncertainty. When war, sanctions, or sudden industrial surges—such as China's 2000s urbanization boom—fracture normal trade routes, traders step in with chartered ships, storage tanks, and rapid credit lines, preventing local shortages from cascading into broader supply breakdowns.

That arbitrage function creates staggering value when markets tighten. As Blas and Farchy document, in the decade to 2011, the top three trading houses in oil, metals, and agriculture—Vitol, Glencore, and Cargill—earned a combined $76.3 billion in net income, ten times their 1990s profits and more than either Apple or Coca-Cola made over the same period.

Argue Against

Concentrating global resource flows inside lightly regulated private partnerships carries steep geopolitical and governance costs. Because traders profit from friction and historically operated in hubs with minimal disclosure, their willingness to finance cash-strapped governments—through oil-backed prepayment loans—can blunt diplomatic sanctions, prolong civil conflicts, and shield authoritarian regimes from accountability.

The same secrecy that lets traders move fast also breeds systemic corruption risks. Vitol paid roughly $164 million in 2020 and Glencore agreed to pay roughly $1.5 billion globally in 2022 to settle U.S., UK, and Brazilian charges involving foreign bribery and market manipulation. Moreover, since Glencore's 2011 IPO and the rise of state-backed trading desks, critics note that tighter scrutiny has narrowed the old era of unchallenged super-profits.

“By exploiting these price differences, they help to make markets more efficient, directing resources to their highest value uses in response to price signals. They are, in the words of one academic, the visible manifestation of Adam Smith's invisible hand.”

— Javier Blas and Jack Farchy, The World for Sale (2021), Introduction

STRATEGY · FT 2023 SHORTLIST

Think slow, act fast—and build from modular blocks

How Big Things Get Done: The Surprising Factors That Determine the Fate of Every Project, from Home Renovations to Space Exploration, and Everything in Between · Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner
2026-11-23 Entry ↗
512 words

Core Idea

Most large endeavors fail long before execution begins—they start wrong. Drawing on a global database of over 16,000 projects across 136 countries and more than 20 fields, Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner uncover what they call the "Iron Law of Megaprojects": over budget, over time, under benefits, over and over again. Only 8.5% of projects finish on budget and on time, and just 0.5% deliver on budget, schedule, and promised benefits. Worse, cost overruns are "fat-tailed," meaning extreme blowouts happen far more often than standard risk models expect.

To beat the Iron Law, Flyvbjerg and Gardner prescribe two interlocking rules: "think slow, act fast" and "find your Lego." Planning is cheap and safe, whereas active delivery burns capital daily and opens what the authors call the "window of doom"—exposure to political, market, and supply shocks. Rigorous upfront simulation and design let teams compress delivery into a fast, disciplined sprint, while breaking bespoke megaprojects into standardized, repeatable modules turns one-off gambles into steep learning curves.

Example from the Book

In 1929, developers John Raskob and Al Smith set out to build the Empire State Building—then the world's tallest skyscraper—on a brutal schedule. Rather than rushing crews onto the site to improvise, architects Shreve, Lamb & Harmon and builders Starrett Brothers & Eken spent months mapping every structural detail on paper, standardizing floor plans, steel beams, exterior stone panels, and window frames into repeatable units.

Once tower construction began in March 1930, the steel frame rose averaging roughly 4.5 stories a week, and the skyscraper went from initial sketch to completion in 21 months (about 13 months of main tower construction). Even with Depression-era price deflation lending a tailwind, disciplined upfront design paid off: budgeted at $50 million, the tower opened ahead of schedule at roughly $41 million—about 17–18% under budget.

Argue For

Treating planning as cheap experimentation and delivery as a fast assembly line directly attacks fat-tailed risk. Iterating on paper, in software models, or with pilot modules costs pennies compared to fixing mistakes mid-build, when crews sit idle and financing charges compound.

Flyvbjerg's Appendix A data confirms the payoff of repeatability: modular technologies such as solar farms (2% suffering 50%+ overruns, +1% mean overrun) and wind power (7% in the tail, +13% mean) stay close to budget, whereas bespoke nuclear plants (55% in the tail, +120% mean) and Olympic Games (76% in the tail, +157% mean) routinely spiral.

Argue Against

Excessive upfront planning can slide into analysis paralysis when user needs or underlying technologies shift faster than a static blueprint can anticipate. In fast-moving software or exploratory R&D, spending a year perfecting specifications before shipping risks building the wrong product cleanly.

Modularity also has real physical and strategic limits. Not every landmark or frontier infrastructure system can be stamped from identical Lego bricks—and occasionally, as with the Sydney Opera House (roughly 1,400% over budget and 10 years late, yet a UNESCO World Heritage icon), society judges a bespoke creation by cultural breakthroughs that cost-benefit tables miss.

“Put enormous care and effort into planning to ensure that delivery is smooth and swift. Think slow, act fast: That's the secret of success.”

— Bent Flyvbjerg & Dan Gardner, How Big Things Get Done (2023), Chapter 1

STRATEGY · FT 2019 SHORTLIST

Broad analogical range unlocks wicked problems

Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World · David Epstein
2026-11-20 Entry ↗
514 words

Core Idea

Early hyper-specialization works wonders in what psychologist Robin Hogarth called "kind" learning environments—domains like chess, golf, or classical music where rules are fixed, patterns repeat, and feedback is rapid and unambiguous. Most consequential decisions in business, engineering, and science, however, unfold in "wicked" environments, where rules shift, patterns rarely repeat cleanly, and feedback is delayed or actively misleading.

In Range, David Epstein shows that in wicked domains, drilling ever deeper into a narrow silo traps thinkers in surface-level experience. Breakthroughs instead depend on "far transfer"—recognizing deep structural analogies across distant fields. People who spend an early "sampling period" exploring multiple domains accumulate a wider library of mental models, letting them import solutions from outside a problem's home turf rather than mistaking yesterday's local rules for permanent laws.

Example from the Book

In Chapter 5, Epstein highlights a classic cognitive psychology experiment by Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak (1980) built around Karl Duncker's tumor-radiation puzzle: a doctor must destroy an inoperable stomach tumor using high-intensity rays that would also kill healthy tissue on the way in. The elegant answer is to split the beam into many weak rays fired from different angles so they converge at full strength only on the tumor.

Presented cold, only about 10% of participants found that solution. Next, Gick and Holyoak gave a new group a seemingly unrelated warm-up story first—about a general who splits his army into small squads along separate mined roads so they converge safely on a fortress at once—before asking them to solve the tumor puzzle. Without a prompt to connect the two, only roughly 30% spotted the structural parallel; when given an explicit hint that the earlier story might help, roughly 75% solved it. Even when the right answer sits right in front of us, narrow surface framing ("medicine" vs. "military") blinds us unless we actively reason across domains.

Argue For

Real-world strategy and R&D rarely resemble chess; once the frontier moves, yesterday's narrow playbook turns into a blindfold. Broad exploration equips teams with conceptual breadth to spot structural parallels when familiar rules break down.

Empirical innovation data backs up that outside-in edge. In a study highlighted by Epstein, Lars Bo Jeppesen and Karim Lakhani examined 166 stubborn corporate R&D problems posted to the open-innovation platform InnoCentive; outside contributors solved 49 of them (nearly 30%), and the further a problem sat from a solver's primary specialty, the higher their odds of cracking it.

Argue Against

Range is a complement to depth, not a license for superficial dabbling. In high-precision bottlenecks with tight feedback loops—such as neurosurgery, compiler design, or semiconductor lithography—world-class execution still requires thousands of hours of domain-specific mastery that generalists cannot shortcut.

Delaying specialization also carries real organizational and career friction: broad explorers can look unfocused on traditional resumes, struggle in specialist hiring screens, and need deliberate synthesis so wide exposure compounds into sound judgment rather than scattered trivia.

“Facing kind problems, narrow specialization can be remarkably efficient. The problem is that we often expect the hyperspecialist, because of their expertise in a narrow area, to magically be able to extend their skill to wicked problems. The results can be disastrous.”

— David Epstein, Range (2019), Chapter 10

GEOPOLITICS · FT 2022 WINNER

Hyper-specialization creates geopolitical chokepoints

Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology · Chris Miller
2026-11-19 Entry ↗
578 words

Core Idea

Modern globalization was supposed to make supply chains resilient by spreading production across borders. In Chip War, economic historian Chris Miller shows that in semiconductors, globalization did the exact opposite: it created extreme geographic and corporate chokepoints.

Building leading-edge microchips requires such staggering capital investment and compounding engineering know-how that no single country—and almost no single company—can master every link alone. As the industry split into specialized niches across continents (design software in the U.S., ultra-precise optics in Germany, lithography scanners in the Netherlands, advanced fabrication in Taiwan and South Korea), competitive markets quietly collapsed into near-monopolies at each step. Whoever controls an irreplaceable bottleneck holds disproportionate geopolitical leverage—not just over smartphones and AI data centers, but over modern military power itself.

Example from the Book

In 1987, veteran Texas Instruments executive Morris Chang founded TSMC in Hsinchu, Taiwan—backed by the Taiwanese government (~48%) and Dutch electronics giant Philips (~27.6% alongside key IP transfers)—around a radical business model: the pure-play foundry. At the time, chipmakers like Intel both designed and fabricated their own chips. Chang promised TSMC would never design or sell its own branded silicon, eliminating any conflict of interest with customers.

That trust unlocked the fabless boom: companies like Nvidia, Qualcomm, and Apple could pour capital into chip architecture while outsourcing manufacturing to TSMC. By pooling global demand, TSMC reinvested profits into ever-smaller nodes faster than integrated rivals could match, eventually fabricating over 90% of the world's most advanced (sub-10nm) logic chips. Yet TSMC itself depends on a single European chokepoint: Dutch firm ASML, the sole maker of Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines, which blast molten tin droplets with lasers inside a vacuum and bounce 13.5-nanometer light off Carl Zeiss mirrors—all to print designs created on software dominated by U.S. firms Synopsys and Cadence (alongside Siemens EDA, formerly U.S. firm Mentor Graphics).

Argue For

Hyper-specialization is the only reason computing kept getting exponentially faster and cheaper. Modern leading-edge fabs cost $10B–$20B+ each, standard EUV scanners run roughly $150M–$200M, and next-generation High-NA EUV machines reach $350M–$400M. Only by aggregating global demand at TSMC and pooling multi-decade R&D across the U.S., Europe, and East Asia could the industry sustain Moore's Law at consumer prices.

Strategically, proponents of this interdependence also point to Taiwan's 'Silicon Shield': because a disruption in Hsinchu would cripple both Western and Chinese tech supply chains simultaneously, Taiwan's chokepoint status raises the economic cost of military conflict.

Argue Against

Optimizing purely for cost efficiency builds catastrophic single points of failure. Concentrating over 90% of leading-edge logic fabrication on an island roughly 100 miles off mainland China's coast turns a regional geopolitical flashpoint—or a major earthquake—into a systemic threat to the global economy. Worse, once bottlenecks harden into monopolies, they invite weaponization: Washington moved early to block ASML EUV shipments to China (2019–2020) and later expanded controls to U.S. chip-design tools and advanced AI accelerators.

As Miller himself warns, relying on a 'Silicon Shield' assumes economic deterrence always overrides political ambition—just as Europe once assumed Russian gas interdependence would prevent war. Recognizing that fragility, the U.S. passed the $52.7 billion CHIPS and Science Act in 2022—alongside parallel subsidy drives in the EU and Japan—explicitly trading some cost efficiency to rebuild domestic redundancy.

“Unlike oil, which can be bought from many countries, our production of computing power depends fundamentally on a series of choke points: tools, chemicals, and software that often are produced by a handful of companies—and sometimes only by one. No other facet of the economy is so dependent on so few firms.”

— Chris Miller, Chip War (2022)
