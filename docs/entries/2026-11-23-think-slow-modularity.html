Think slow, act fast—and build from modular blocks

How Big Things Get Done: The Surprising Factors That Determine the Fate of Every Project, from Home Renovations to Space Exploration, and Everything in Between · Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner
#strategy #project-management #modularity #risk #ft-2023-shortlist
2026-11-23

Core Idea

Most large endeavors fail long before execution begins—they start wrong. Drawing on a global database of over 16,000 projects across 136 countries and more than 20 fields, Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner uncover what they call the "Iron Law of Megaprojects": over budget, over time, under benefits, over and over again. Only 8.5% of projects finish on budget and on time, and just 0.5% deliver on budget, schedule, and promised benefits. Worse, cost overruns are "fat-tailed," meaning extreme blowouts happen far more often than standard risk models expect.

To beat the Iron Law, Flyvbjerg and Gardner prescribe two interlocking rules: "think slow, act fast" and "find your Lego." Planning is cheap and safe, whereas active delivery burns capital daily and opens what the authors call the "window of doom"—exposure to political, market, and supply shocks. Rigorous upfront simulation and design let teams compress delivery into a fast, disciplined sprint, while breaking bespoke megaprojects into standardized, repeatable modules turns one-off gambles into steep learning curves.

Example from the Book

In 1929, developers John Raskob and Al Smith set out to build the Empire State Building—then the world's tallest skyscraper—on a brutal schedule. Rather than rushing crews onto the site to improvise, architects Shreve, Lamb & Harmon and builders Starrett Brothers & Eken spent months mapping every structural detail on paper, standardizing floor plans, steel beams, exterior stone panels, and window frames into repeatable units.

Once tower construction began in March 1930, the steel frame rose averaging roughly 4.5 stories a week, and the skyscraper went from initial sketch to completion in 21 months (about 13 months of main tower construction). Even with Depression-era price deflation lending a tailwind, disciplined upfront design paid off: budgeted at $50 million, the tower opened ahead of schedule at roughly $41 million—about 17–18% under budget.

Argue For

Treating planning as cheap experimentation and delivery as a fast assembly line directly attacks fat-tailed risk. Iterating on paper, in software models, or with pilot modules costs pennies compared to fixing mistakes mid-build, when crews sit idle and financing charges compound.

Flyvbjerg's Appendix A data confirms the payoff of repeatability: modular technologies such as solar farms (2% suffering 50%+ overruns, +1% mean overrun) and wind power (7% in the tail, +13% mean) stay close to budget, whereas bespoke nuclear plants (55% in the tail, +120% mean) and Olympic Games (76% in the tail, +157% mean) routinely spiral.

Argue Against

Excessive upfront planning can slide into analysis paralysis when user needs or underlying technologies shift faster than a static blueprint can anticipate. In fast-moving software or exploratory R&D, spending a year perfecting specifications before shipping risks building the wrong product cleanly.

Modularity also has real physical and strategic limits. Not every landmark or frontier infrastructure system can be stamped from identical Lego bricks—and occasionally, as with the Sydney Opera House (roughly 1,400% over budget and 10 years late, yet a UNESCO World Heritage icon), society judges a bespoke creation by cultural breakthroughs that cost-benefit tables miss.

“Put enormous care and effort into planning to ensure that delivery is smooth and swift. Think slow, act fast: That's the secret of success.”

— Bent Flyvbjerg & Dan Gardner, How Big Things Get Done (2023), Chapter 1
