STRATEGY · FT 2019 SHORTLIST

Broad analogical range unlocks wicked problems

Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World · David Epstein
#strategy #problem-solving #generalists #innovation #ft-2019-shortlist
2026-11-20
514 words

Core Idea

Early hyper-specialization works wonders in what psychologist Robin Hogarth called "kind" learning environments—domains like chess, golf, or classical music where rules are fixed, patterns repeat, and feedback is rapid and unambiguous. Most consequential decisions in business, engineering, and science, however, unfold in "wicked" environments, where rules shift, patterns rarely repeat cleanly, and feedback is delayed or actively misleading.

In Range, David Epstein shows that in wicked domains, drilling ever deeper into a narrow silo traps thinkers in surface-level experience. Breakthroughs instead depend on "far transfer"—recognizing deep structural analogies across distant fields. People who spend an early "sampling period" exploring multiple domains accumulate a wider library of mental models, letting them import solutions from outside a problem's home turf rather than mistaking yesterday's local rules for permanent laws.

Example from the Book

In Chapter 5, Epstein highlights a classic cognitive psychology experiment by Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak (1980) built around Karl Duncker's tumor-radiation puzzle: a doctor must destroy an inoperable stomach tumor using high-intensity rays that would also kill healthy tissue on the way in. The elegant answer is to split the beam into many weak rays fired from different angles so they converge at full strength only on the tumor.

Presented cold, only about 10% of participants found that solution. Next, Gick and Holyoak gave a new group a seemingly unrelated warm-up story first—about a general who splits his army into small squads along separate mined roads so they converge safely on a fortress at once—before asking them to solve the tumor puzzle. Without a prompt to connect the two, only roughly 30% spotted the structural parallel; when given an explicit hint that the earlier story might help, roughly 75% solved it. Even when the right answer sits right in front of us, narrow surface framing ("medicine" vs. "military") blinds us unless we actively reason across domains.

Argue For

Real-world strategy and R&D rarely resemble chess; once the frontier moves, yesterday's narrow playbook turns into a blindfold. Broad exploration equips teams with conceptual breadth to spot structural parallels when familiar rules break down.

Empirical innovation data backs up that outside-in edge. In a study highlighted by Epstein, Lars Bo Jeppesen and Karim Lakhani examined 166 stubborn corporate R&D problems posted to the open-innovation platform InnoCentive; outside contributors solved 49 of them (nearly 30%), and the further a problem sat from a solver's primary specialty, the higher their odds of cracking it.

Argue Against

Range is a complement to depth, not a license for superficial dabbling. In high-precision bottlenecks with tight feedback loops—such as neurosurgery, compiler design, or semiconductor lithography—world-class execution still requires thousands of hours of domain-specific mastery that generalists cannot shortcut.

Delaying specialization also carries real organizational and career friction: broad explorers can look unfocused on traditional resumes, struggle in specialist hiring screens, and need deliberate synthesis so wide exposure compounds into sound judgment rather than scattered trivia.

“Facing kind problems, narrow specialization can be remarkably efficient. The problem is that we often expect the hyperspecialist, because of their expertise in a narrow area, to magically be able to extend their skill to wicked problems. The results can be disastrous.”

— David Epstein, Range (2019), Chapter 10
